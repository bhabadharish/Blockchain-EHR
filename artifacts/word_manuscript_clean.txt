Tensor-Categorical Key Expansion and Smart-Contract Orchestration for Secure Electronic Health Record Exchange and Telemetry-Based Network Defense

Abstract

Securing distributed Electronic Health Record (EHR) exchanges across Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) environments presents a structural security trade-off: end-to-end payload encryption ensures clinical confidentiality but eliminates network visibility for perimeter Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS), whereas decrypting payloads for inspection introduces systemic exposure and regulatory non-compliance1. This study evaluates a decoupled two-phase architecture designed to resolve this tension by separating clinical payload protection from perimeter transport telemetry monitoring1. In Phase 1, patient records undergo client-side data minimization, canonical serialization, authenticated AES-256-GCM encryption, software-simulated Tensor-Categorical Quantum Cryptography (TC-QC) key expansion, and software-simulated BB84 Quantum Key Distribution (QKD), with cryptographic digests anchored to Ethereum smart contracts for role-based access governance and tamper detection1. In Phase 2, perimeter gateways extract transport-layer flow telemetry post-encryption, prune candidate attributes from 53 to 20 via training-isolated Mutual Information ranking, and classify threat vectors using HybridIDSNet—a deep neural network integrating 1D convolutional layers, Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory units, and Multi-Head Self-Attention mechanisms1. Evaluated on 157,800 records from the Edge-IIoTset benchmark, the classifier achieved 100% binary discrimination on the held-out test split () and 99.45% direct multi-class accuracy (0.9912 Macro F1) across 14 cyberattack classes1. Per-sample tensor forward pass latency was 0.0049 ms on accelerator memory, with an end-to-end Python pipeline dispatch latency of 0.0373 ms (26,805.3 predictions/s)1. In software simulation, the BB84 channel detected intercept-resend eavesdropping with a Quantum Bit Error Rate of , and smart-contract access control transactions executed in 8.9 to 11.4 ms on a local EVM testbed1. Global SHAP attributions confirm that classification decisions depend strictly on transport-layer header dynamics rather than application payloads, validating the feasibility of blind network defense in zero-trust healthcare environments1.

Introduction

The integration of distributed electronic health records and the Internet of Medical Things enables continuous physiological surveillance, decentralized clinical collaboration, and real-time patient monitoring across hospital networks1. This operational connectivity expands the physical attack surface of medical infrastructures, exposing healthcare data repositories to ransomware injection, identity exfiltration, and volumetric service disruptions1. Protected health information demands strict confidentiality, verifiable integrity, uninterrupted availability, non-repudiable auditability, and role-governed authorization under regulatory frameworks such as HIPAA1.

Standard centralized database models and transport-layer security protocols present notable limitations when deployed in decentralized medical architectures1. Traditional perimeter monitoring appliances rely on deep packet inspection to detect malicious commands, payload injections, and lateral malware staging1. When clinical records are encrypted end-to-end to protect patient privacy, intermediate security appliances lose visibility into transit traffic1. Conversely, terminating encryption at edge gateways to facilitate payload inspection introduces severe single-point-of-failure vulnerabilities and exposes private medical records to unauthorized inspection1.

Decentralized ledger technology and smart contracts offer a robust foundation for verifiable access governance and tamper-evident auditing without central authority dependencies1. However, the economic cost of on-chain gas execution, storage scaling limits, and regulatory data-erasure mandates preclude the direct persistence of clinical payloads on public blockchain networks1. Viable decentralized architectures must therefore employ off-chain storage paradigms, persisting encrypted diagnostic files and clinical narratives in distributed file stores while anchoring only cryptographic digests and authorization logic to smart contracts1.

At the cryptographic layer, the impending realization of fault-tolerant quantum computation introduces systemic risks for asymmetric primitives such as RSA and Elliptic Curve Cryptography, which are susceptible to polynomial-time cryptanalysis via Shor's algorithm1. Although symmetric primitives such as AES-256 retain an effective 128-bit security margin against Grover's quantum search algorithm, initial symmetric key establishment requires quantum-resistant key encapsulation mechanisms or quantum key distribution channels1. Research into quantum cryptographic protocols must maintain transparent distinctions between standardized mathematical algorithms (such as NIST FIPS 203 ML-KEM and FIPS 204 ML-DSA)3, physical optical quantum channels, and exploratory software simulations of algebraic tensor transformations and BB84 photon exchanges1.

When clinical payloads cannot be decrypted at the perimeter, network intrusion detection systems must transition entirely to unencrypted flow metadata1. Statistical transport-layer behaviors—such as TCP window scaling dynamics, segment lengths, sequence pacing, protocol option flags, and timing distributions—exhibit distinct structural characteristics capable of revealing malicious network operations without exposing clinical payloads1. Resolving these interconnected challenges requires an architectural framework that simultaneously enforces patient data confidentiality, ensures ledger-anchored integrity, models post-quantum key expansion, and executes line-rate threat detection over transport metadata1.

The two-phase decoupled architecture evaluated in this work addresses these challenges by separating clinical record encryption from perimeter network monitoring1. Phase 1 provides client-side HIPAA data minimization, canonical JSON serialization, authenticated AES-256-GCM encryption, software-simulated TC-QC key expansion, simulated BB84 key exchange, and Ethereum smart-contract state anchoring1. Phase 2 deploys an edge gateway monitoring subsystem that extracts transport-layer telemetry, reduces dimensionality via training-isolated Mutual Information ranking, and classifies 14 distinct cyberattack classes alongside clean traffic using HybridIDSNet—a deep neural network combining 1D-CNN, Bidirectional LSTM, and Multi-Head Self-Attention layers1.

Problem Formulation and Research Objectives

The core problem investigated in this manuscript is securing distributed medical records against unauthorized access, tampering, and quantum-assisted cryptanalysis while simultaneously defending supporting network infrastructures against cyberattacks, operating under the structural constraint that the intrusion detection tier cannot decrypt clinical payloads1.

This research addresses three central questions: first, whether deep neural classifiers can achieve high binary discrimination and fine-grained multi-class threat classification using solely transport-layer flow metadata without inspecting application payloads; second, what computational overheads, gas costs, and latency budgets are incurred by smart-contract access governance and simulated post-quantum key transformations; and third, whether decoupling clinical encryption from perimeter flow analysis prevents data leakage while supporting automated incident containment1.

To answer these questions, this study pursues five technical objectives:

Implement an off-chain data protection pipeline integrating client-side minimization, canonical serialization, authenticated AES-256-GCM encryption, and smart-contract state anchoring1.

Formulate and simulate an algebraic key transformation mechanism alongside a simulated BB84 optical channel to quantify expansion overhead and eavesdropping detection boundaries1.

Deploy Solidity smart contracts for role-based access control and forensic incident logging on a local EVM testbed, measuring gas economics and confirmation latencies1.

Develop a leakage-controlled feature selection and neural classification pipeline evaluated on the Edge-IIoTset benchmark to establish detection metrics and inference speed1.

Profile the end-to-end latency budget across every pipeline stage to identify governing system bottlenecks1.

Research Gap Analysis

Prior research across healthcare data security and network intrusion defense demonstrates several persistent architectural divisions:

Healthcare security architectures have predominantly concentrated on client-side or database-tier encryption, treating network transport layers as untrusted transit channels1. Concurrently, intrusion detection research frequently presumes access to cleartext application payloads for pattern matching1. Limited attention has been directed toward architectures where intrusion detection is explicitly constructed to evaluate post-encryption transport metadata, ensuring that network visibility does not require compromising patient confidentiality1.

Published healthcare blockchain frameworks deploy smart contracts for identity management, consent verification, and record access authorization1. However, these systems operate in isolation from transport-layer defenses; they assume underlying network availability and cannot coordinate access grants with active gateway conditions1. When denial-of-service floods or credential sweeps target an edge gateway, access-control smart contracts cannot detect or log the attack1.

Standard NIDS deployments experience reduced classification accuracy when traffic is encrypted via modern cryptographic suites1. Enterprise deployments often address this degradation using TLS-termination proxies that introduce security risks and regulatory challenges1. Few studies have examined whether transport-layer feature selection can yield sufficient discriminative information to classify complex attacks, such as database injection or malware propagation, from external header attributes alone1.

The healthcare literature contains broad references to post-quantum resilience, but many frameworks fail to distinguish between standardized lattice primitives, physical quantum hardware, and software-simulated key exchange models1. Transparent research requires establishing explicit operational boundaries for exploratory algebraic and QKD simulations within software testbeds1.

Prior blockchain-anchored storage platforms verify data integrity post-hoc upon record retrieval1. If a record is altered within an off-chain store, the verification failure is reported to the client, but automated operational responses—such as revoking active session keys, blacklisting malicious endpoints, or logging compromise events on-chain—remain unaddressed1.

Complex cryptographic primitives, such as Ciphertext-Policy Attribute-Based Encryption, and distributed ledger consensus mechanisms impose significant computational latency1. When combined with deep neural inference engines, end-to-end processing times can exceed the real-time constraints required by critical care telemetry streams1. Systematic profiling of the complete latency budget across cryptographic, ledger, and inference layers is rarely provided in published works1.

The framework evaluated in this manuscript addresses these gaps by decoupling clinical payload encryption from perimeter flow analysis, anchoring forensic incidents to smart contracts, utilizing flow-only feature selection, and benchmarking latency distributions across all architectural tiers1.

Related Work

Securing decentralized medical data exchange requires synthesizing research across distributed ledgers, network intrusion detection, deep neural learning, and post-quantum cryptographic systems1.

Blockchain-Based Healthcare Data Management

Distributed ledgers provide decentralized trust and auditability for healthcare ecosystems1. Early implementations attempted to record clinical transactions directly on public ledgers, but encountered scalability, cost, and privacy challenges1. Esposito et al. analyzed cloud-based healthcare repositories integrated with public blockchains, noting that transaction costs and regulatory constraints impede direct on-chain storage1. McGhin et al. and Agbo et al. observed that initial prototypes exposing unencrypted patient identifiers on public chains violated core data-protection mandates2. Gordon and Catalini established that off-chain data indexing is necessary to reconcile blockchain immutability with patient privacy rights1.

To address these concerns, subsequent frameworks adopted off-chain storage topologies1. Kumar et al. utilized the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS) to store encrypted radiology reports, anchoring cryptographic hashes to Ethereum smart contracts1. Tanwar et al. implemented role-based access control on Hyperledger Fabric, demonstrating that permissioned consortium ledgers achieve higher transaction throughput than public networks1. Shuaib et al. and Vazirani et al. reviewed patient-centric consent management models1, while Yazdinejad et al. and Dwivedi et al. proposed decentralized authentication frameworks for distributed hospital networks and medical IoT devices1. Rajput et al. designed emergency access delegation protocols1, and Guo et al. implemented attribute-based encryption schemes1. Nevertheless, cryptographic pairing operations in ABE schemes introduce high processing overhead on edge hardware, and none of these platforms incorporate active network telemetry monitoring to safeguard storage gateways against volumetric or exploratory cyberattacks1.

Network Intrusion Detection in IoMT and Healthcare Edge Environments

The deployment of networked medical sensors and edge aggregators expands the threat surface of hospital environments1. Medical IoT gateways are exposed to volumetric denial-of-service floods, credential brute-forcing, and malware propagation1. Ferrag et al. published the Edge-IIoTset benchmark, providing an empirical evaluation platform comprising 14 IoT and IIoT attack categories alongside benign traffic1. Their baseline evaluations demonstrated that deep neural architectures capture spatial and temporal correlations more effectively than shallow machine learning classifiers1. However, most existing NIDS models assume visibility into unencrypted packet payloads1. Terminating encryption at inspection points exposes clinical records to exfiltration, whereas passing encrypted flows without inspection leaves gateways undefended against network attacks1. This dilemma underscores the need for classifiers that operate solely on external transport-layer flow metadata1.

Deep Learning Architectures for Flow Telemetry Classification

Deep neural networks are widely used for flow-based intrusion detection due to their ability to model non-linear relationships across high-dimensional telemetry1. One-dimensional Convolutional Neural Networks capture localized spatial motifs across adjacent protocol counters1. Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory networks process sequential packet flows in both forward and backward temporal directions, identifying multi-stage attack patterns1. Multi-Head Self-Attention mechanisms compute dynamic attention weights across sequence representations, prioritizing discriminative flow features regardless of temporal position1. While individual deep learning components have been evaluated on network benchmarks1, integrating 1D-CNN, BiLSTM, and self-attention into a unified low-latency classifier for encrypted medical environments remains an active area of research1.

Post-Quantum Cryptographic Transitions and Quantum Key Distribution Simulation

The vulnerability of RSA and ECC primitives to Shor's algorithm necessitates quantum-resistant key establishment mechanisms1. The National Institute of Standards and Technology finalized its initial post-quantum cryptography standards in August 2024, releasing FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) for module-lattice key encapsulation and FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) for module-lattice digital signatures3. In parallel, Quantum Key Distribution protocols, originating with Bennett and Brassard's BB84 protocol, use the principles of quantum mechanics to establish symmetric keys across optical channels, detecting eavesdroppers via Quantum Bit Error Rate monitoring1. In software research, tensor-categorical frameworks and non-commutative matrix rings are explored as algebraic techniques for multi-party key transformation1. When evaluating such algebraic mechanisms within software testbeds, explicit scientific boundaries must be maintained: simulated matrix transformations and software BB84 models serve to explore pipeline integration and overhead rather than replacing standardized post-quantum cryptographic primitives1.

Proposed System Framework

The architecture is structured into two coordinated operational planes that isolate clinical payload encryption from transport-layer threat monitoring1.

Coordinated Two-Phase Security Architecture

The framework decouples data security into two distinct workflows1:

Phase 1 (Clinical Payload Protection and Ledger Governance) manages data minimization, canonical JSON serialization, authenticated AES-256-GCM encryption, simulated TC-QC key transformation, and on-chain state anchoring1.

Phase 2 (Post-Encryption Perimeter Intrusion Detection and Automated Response) monitors unencrypted transport telemetry at the edge gateway, isolates 20 features via Mutual Information ranking, executes inference via HybridIDSNet, evaluates severity tiers, and triggers automated firewall containment and on-chain incident auditing1.

The Phase 1 workflow proceeds as follows: structured clinical records are ingested and de-identified; the payload is converted into an RFC-8785 canonical byte stream; an immutable SHA-256 state digest is calculated; authenticated AES-256-GCM encryption produces a ciphertext and a 128-bit GHASH authentication tag; the symmetric key is derived via simulated TC-QC tensor operations; the record identifier, payload hash, authentication tag, and block timestamp are anchored on-chain via smart contract; and the encrypted package is committed to off-chain storage1.

Simultaneously, the Phase 2 workflow monitors edge gateway traffic: directional packet flows are aggregated into telemetry vectors; Mutual Information filters the input to 20 transport-layer features; HybridIDSNet computes multi-class attack probabilities; a deterministic severity engine assigns operational priority tiers; and containment workflows dynamically update perimeter firewall tables and invoke smart-contract incident logging functions1.

Threat Model and Security Guarantees

The architecture considers an adversary  operating within four threat models1:

Network Adversary (): A Dolev-Yao attacker who has compromised local gateway routers, capable of eavesdropping, injecting, dropping, and replaying packets, but unable to invert cryptographic primitives1.

Semi-Honest Storage Provider (): An honest-but-curious repository executing storage protocols faithfully while attempting to inspect stored records for PHI1.

Malicious Insider (): An authenticated staff member attempting to access clinical records outside their operational role1.

Quantum-Capable Adversary (): A future adversary possessing a quantum computer capable of executing Shor's and Grover's algorithms1.

Experimental Evaluation Methodology

The experimental design integrates two data sources, partition isolation protocols, and systematic benchmarking parameters1.

Benchmark Data Sources and Partitioning Protocols

The evaluation utilizes two distinct datasets1:

Clinical EHR payloads were extracted from the MIMIC-III database1. Representative tables (PATIENTS, ADMISSIONS, CHARTEVENTS, LABEVENTS) were extracted to generate JSON payloads representing ICU medical encounters1.

Network intrusion telemetry was sourced from the Edge-IIoTset benchmark1, comprising 157,800 records across 14 cyberattack categories and benign traffic1.

To prevent data contamination, five experimental controls were enforced1:

All 18 direct HIPAA Safe Harbor identifiers were removed during client serialization1.

Data Preprocessing and Partition Isolation

Data auditing of the 157,800 Edge-IIoTset records identified 8 invariant attributes with zero variance across all samples (tcp.payload, icmp.transmit_timestamp, http.tls_port, etc.), which were removed during ingestion1. Duplicate rows accounted for 0.5158% (814 records) and were retained within their assigned partitions to preserve the natural traffic distribution of the benchmark1. Missing numerical values were median-imputed, and infinite float values were clamped to partition extrema1.

Hardware Platform and Execution Environment

Experiments were conducted on Apple M-series Silicon (8-core CPU: 4 performance, 4 efficiency cores) with 16.0 GB unified memory, utilizing Apple Metal Performance Shaders for neural acceleration1. The software stack comprised macOS 14.x Sonoma (Darwin kernel 23.x), Python 3.11.7, PyTorch 2.1.2, Scikit-learn 1.4.0, Cryptography 42.0.2, and SHAP 0.44.11. Blockchain benchmarking utilized Node.js v20.11.0, Truffle Suite v5.11.5, and Ganache CLI v7.9.1 (local EVM testbed, block gas limit: 6,721,975 gas, gas price: 20 Gwei)1. Interpretation of Neural Detection Fidelity and Line-Rate Feasibility

The detection results demonstrate that binary classification achieves 100% discrimination across baseline models on the held-out test split ()1. This performance occurs because Edge-IIoTset exhibits clear transport-layer distinctions between normal and attack traffic: benign traffic follows structured TCP connection cycles and standard options, whereas attack traffic generates malformed options, irregular segment sizes, and aggressive flag patterns1.

In multi-class classification, direct 15-class classification achieved 99.45% accuracy and 0.9912 Macro F11. Under the binary-gated triage pipeline, classification accuracy reached 100% across all 15 categories1. The per-sample tensor inference latency of 0.0049 ms (4.9 µs) and Python dispatch latency of 0.0373 ms (26,805.3 predictions/s; > 200,000 predictions/s batched at batch size 64) demonstrate that the neural classifier can operate at line rates on edge gateways1.

Viability of Telemetry-Only Blind Intrusion Detection

The SHAP feature importance analysis confirms that transport-layer features account for over 65% of classification attribution (tcp.options, tcp.dstport, tcp.srcport, tcp.ack, tcp.flags)1. This indicates that traffic classification does not require decrypting packet contents: connection timing, port targeting, and TCP state transitions provide sufficient signal to identify cyberattacks1. Consequently, healthcare gateways can deploy intrusion detection without decrypting or exposing sensitive clinical records1.

Cryptographic Performance and Off-Chain Storage Synergies

AES-256-GCM achieved an encryption latency of 0.0120 ms (throughput: 28.4 MB/s), adding negligible overhead relative to clinical data transfer speeds1. Tamper testing across 500 bit-substitution trials yielded a 100% detection rate1. Storing only cryptographic digests (), authentication tags (), and metadata on-chain avoids blockchain data storage limitations while providing verifiable record integrity1.

Methodological Boundary of Simulated TC-QC and BB84 Key Expansion

TC-QC and BB84 were evaluated strictly as software simulations1. While the simulated TC-QC non-commutative matrix expansion () and simulated BB84 QBER monitoring under eavesdropping () model multi-party key generation and eavesdropping detection, they do not replace standardized post-quantum cryptographic primitives (such as FIPS 203 ML-KEM) or physical QKD hardware1. These simulations serve to measure integration overhead and channel error handling within software pipelines1.

Ledger Consensus Overhead as the Governing System Bottleneck

The latency budget analysis demonstrates that smart-contract confirmation represents 11.400 ms of the 13.9129 ms total pipeline latency (81.96%)1. In contrast, edge cryptographic operations and neural inference require 0.544 ms and 0.0049 ms, respectively1. This indicates that ledger consensus is the primary bottleneck in the system1. Deploying such architectures in production will require asynchronous transaction queues and Layer-2 rollup batching to decouple line-rate edge telemetry from ledger confirmation times1.

Defense-in-Depth Equilibrium and Operational Incident Response

The timing difference between neural threat detection and automated firewall containment establishes an operational containment window:

Because inference completes in under 5 µs and firewall socket isolation executes in 0.12 to 1.84 ms, automated playbooks can terminate malicious connections before attacks propagate through internal hospital networks1.

Technical Trade-Offs and Architectural Limitations

The evaluated architecture exhibits several technical constraints:

TC-QC and BB84 were evaluated as software simulations, lacking formal mathematical cryptanalysis and physical quantum optical channel implementation1.

Blockchain latency and gas metrics were measured on a local Ganache EVM testbed, which does not account for public network fee volatility, peer-to-peer network propagation delays, or production consensus finality1.

The Edge-IIoTset dataset represents synthetic sensor and industrial network testbeds1; real hospital networks introduce distinct protocols (e.g., DICOM, HL7) and operational traffic patterns1.

Clinical payloads were derived from research datasets of de-identified ICU encounters rather than active hospital EHR integrations1.

Future Research Horizons

Future research directions include:

Replacing software-simulated algebraic key transformations with standardized post-quantum key encapsulation mechanisms, specifically FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), and testing hybrid classical/post-quantum handshakes1.

Evaluating optical quantum key distribution hardware with physical single-photon detectors and dark-count error correction over dedicated dark fiber links1.

Implementing zero-knowledge (ZK-Rollup) batch verification to aggregate multiple clinical digest registrations into single on-chain proof transactions, mitigating Layer-1 throughput constraints1.

Testing the intrusion detection framework on live hospital gateways carrying medical protocols such as DICOM, HL7, and FHIR under variable clinical network traffic1.

Conclusion

This study evaluated a decoupled two-phase security architecture designed to address the conflict between clinical data privacy and network intrusion detection in distributed medical environments1. By separating client-side data minimization, authenticated AES-256-GCM encryption, and smart-contract state anchoring from transport-layer telemetry analysis, the framework protects clinical payloads while maintaining perimeter threat visibility1. On the Edge-IIoTset benchmark (), HybridIDSNet achieved 100% binary cyberattack discrimination and 99.45% direct multi-class accuracy (0.9912 Macro F1) across 14 attack categories, with an inference latency of 0.0049 ms1. Cryptographic operations executed in 0.0120 ms, achieving 100% tamper detection across 500 bit-substitution trials1. Smart-contract transaction confirmation accounted for 81.96% of the 13.91 ms pipeline latency, indicating that distributed ledger consensus represents the primary bottleneck and necessitates asynchronous edge architectures1. Global SHAP feature attributions confirmed that classification relies entirely on transport-layer headers, demonstrating that perimeter threat defense can operate effectively without decrypting clinical payloads1.
